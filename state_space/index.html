    State Space, Stability, and Linearization¶

    A robot arm has dozens of coupled equations of motion, so we need one uniform form that software can handle. That form is state space.

    From one equation to many¶

    Give every derivative except the highest its own name. For the mass-spring-damper, let \(x_1 = x\) (position) and \(x_2 = \dot{x}\) (velocity):

    \[ \dot{x}_1 = x_2, \qquad \dot{x}_2 = -\frac{k}{m}x_1 - \frac{b}{m}x_2 + \frac{1}{m}u \]

    An \(N\)th-order equation always becomes \(N\) first-order equations this way.

    The matrix form¶

    Refresher

    A matrix times a vector takes the dot product of each row with the vector. See Vectors and Matrices.

    Stack the states into a vector \(\mathbf{x} = [x_1,\ x_2]^T\) (bold, to tell it apart from the displacement \(x\)):

    \[ \dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\mathbf{x} + Bu, \qquad y = C\mathbf{x} + Du \]
    \[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -k/m & -b/m \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/m \end{bmatrix}, \qquad C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad D = 0 \]
    • \(A\) holds the system's own dynamics.
    • \(B\) says how the input pushes the state.
    • \(C\) picks out the measured output, here the position. \(D\) is a direct path from input to output, usually zero.

    A 7-joint arm needs bigger matrices, but the equation keeps the same shape.

    Stability¶

    Refresher

    The solution of \(\dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\mathbf{x}\) is a sum of modes \(e^{\lambda t}\mathbf{v}\), one per eigenvalue of \(A\). See Differential Equations.

    The eigenvalues of the mass-spring-damper's \(A\) are the roots of

    \[ \det(sI - A) = s^2 + \frac{b}{m}s + \frac{k}{m} = 0 \]

    which is the characteristic equation from the previous page, divided by \(m\). So the eigenvalues of \(A\) are the characteristic roots, and they decide how the system behaves:

    • Every eigenvalue has a negative real part: the system returns to rest. It's stable.
    • Any eigenvalue has a positive real part: some motion grows without bound. It's unstable.
    • Eigenvalues on the imaginary axis, with none to the right: it oscillates forever without growing or decaying, like the undamped spring.

    In the complex plane, stable means every eigenvalue sits in the left half. This figure from Complex Numbers shows the motion each location produces, and that eigenvalues farther left decay faster:

    In code, that's one call:

    #include <Eigen/Dense>

const double m = 1.0, b = 1.0, k = 10.0;
Eigen::Matrix2d A;
A << 0.0,    1.0,
     -k / m, -b / m;
Eigen::EigenSolver<Eigen::Matrix2d> solver(A);
// solver.eigenvalues() = -0.5 ± 3.12j: negative real parts, so stable

    Linearization¶

    Refresher

    Near a point, \(\mathbf{f}(\bar{\mathbf{x}} + \delta\mathbf{x}) \approx \mathbf{f}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}) + J\,\delta\mathbf{x}\), where \(J\) is the Jacobian. See Partial Derivatives and Jacobians.

    Real systems are nonlinear: \(\dot{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}, u)\). Near an equilibrium, a state where \(\mathbf{f} = 0\) and nothing changes, they behave almost linearly, with \(A\) equal to the Jacobian \(\partial\mathbf{f}/\partial\mathbf{x}\) there.

    A pendulum of length \(l\) with mass \(m\) at its tip, pivot friction \(b\), and motor torque \(\tau\) follows

    \[ ml^2\ddot{\theta} + b\dot{\theta} + mgl\sin\theta = \tau \]

    The figure shows both equilibria: hanging down, where gravity pulls it back, and balanced upright, where gravity pulls it away.

    Linearizing around an angle \(\bar{\theta}\) gives

    \[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\dfrac{g}{l}\cos\bar{\theta} & -\dfrac{b}{ml^2} \end{bmatrix} \]
    • Hanging down (\(\bar{\theta} = 0\)): the bottom-left entry is \(-g/l\), just like the spring's \(-k/m\). Gravity acts as a spring, the eigenvalues have negative real parts, and the pendulum is stable.
    • Balanced upright (\(\bar{\theta} = \pi\)): the entry flips to \(+g/l\). The characteristic equation becomes \(s^2 + \frac{b}{ml^2}s - \frac{g}{l} = 0\), which always has one positive real root. The pendulum is unstable: it falls.

    The approximation only holds near the equilibrium, which is why controllers built on it work best when they keep the system close to its operating point.

    Further reading¶

    • Franklin, Powell, and Emami-Naeini, Feedback Control of Dynamic Systems. It writes state space as \(F, G, H, J\) where we use \(A, B, C, D\).
    • Spong, Hutchinson, and Vidyasagar, Robot Modeling and Control, whose notation this course follows.
