    Dynamic Systems¶

    A dynamic system is one whose output depends on its history, not just its current input. Push a cart and let go: it keeps rolling. We describe such systems with differential equations, in terms of an input \(u\) we act on (a force, a torque), an output \(y\) we measure, and a state: the smallest set of variables that, together with future inputs, determines the future.

    First-order systems¶

    A cart of mass \(m\) is pushed with force \(u\) against viscous drag \(-bv\):

    \[ m\dot{v} + bv = u \]

    Refresher

    \(\dot{x} = ax\) has the solution \(x(0)e^{at}\), which decays when \(a < 0\). See Differential Equations.

    With no push, the speed decays as \(e^{-t/\tau}\), where \(\tau = m/b\) is the time constant. With a constant push, the speed rises toward \(u/b\) along the curve in the figure. It covers 63% of the way after one time constant and is essentially done after four.

    Second-order systems¶

    Add a spring and the system can store energy in two places, as motion and as spring stretch, and trade it back and forth. That's what makes oscillation possible.

    In the figure, a block of mass \(m\) is connected to a wall by a spring of stiffness \(k\) and a damper with coefficient \(b\). The spring pulls back with force \(-kx\), the damper resists motion with \(-b\dot{x}\), and we push with \(u\). Newton's second law gives

    \[ m\ddot{x} + b\dot{x} + kx = u \]

    You solved this equation in Differential Equations. Its characteristic roots are clearest when written with two new quantities:

    \[ s = -\zeta\omega_n \pm \omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}, \qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad \zeta = \frac{b}{2\sqrt{km}} \]

    The natural frequency \(\omega_n\) (rad/s) sets how fast the system moves. The damping ratio \(\zeta\) decides what kind of roots it has, and so how the motion dies out:

    Damping ratio Roots Behavior
    \(\zeta = 0\) \(\pm j\omega_n\) Undamped: oscillates forever.
    \(0 < \zeta < 1\) complex pair, \(-\zeta\omega_n \pm j\omega_d\) Underdamped: oscillates with a decaying envelope.
    \(\zeta = 1\) one repeated real root Critically damped: fastest return to rest without overshooting.
    \(\zeta > 1\) two negative real roots Overdamped: creeps back to rest without oscillating.

    Here \(\omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\) is the frequency it actually oscillates at. The figure shows the roots moving as \(\zeta\) increases, with \(\omega_n\) fixed:

    Refresher

    A complex pair \(\sigma \pm j\omega\) gives a solution \(e^{\sigma t}\cos\omega t\): an oscillation at \(\omega\) inside an envelope that decays when \(\sigma < 0\). See Complex Numbers.

    A robot joint under feedback control behaves much like this system, so it's worth building a feel for it.

    Impulse response¶

    An impulse is a sharp strike, like a hammer tap. Its size \(J\) is the force integrated over the strike, and it changes the block's velocity instantly by \(\Delta v = J/m\). The block then rings at \(\omega_d\) inside an envelope that decays at rate \(\zeta\omega_n\).

    The impulse response formula

    Starting from rest, an underdamped block struck with impulse \(J\) moves as

    \[ x(t) = \frac{J}{m\,\omega_d}\, e^{-\zeta\omega_n t} \sin(\omega_d t) \]

    Try it¶

    The block has mass \(m = 1\text{ kg}\), and each press of Impulse adds \(1.5\) m/s to its velocity. Change \(k\) and \(b\) at any time, even mid-swing. (The widget solves the equation numerically; Chapter 5 explains how simulators do this.)

    • Set \(b = 0\). It oscillates forever.
    • For \(k = 10\) N/m, find the \(b\) that gives critical damping.
    • With \(b = 0\), press Impulse in time with the swing. That's resonance.
