    1. Time Domain¶

    The mass-spring-damper¶

    The mass-spring-damper is the "hello world" of dynamic systems. A block of mass \(m\) slides on a frictionless surface. A spring with stiffness \(k\) connects it to a wall, and a damper (think of a shock absorber) with coefficient \(c\) resists its motion.

    Let \(x(t)\) be the block's displacement from its resting position. Two forces act on it:

    • The spring pulls it back toward rest with a force proportional to displacement: \(-kx\).
    • The damper opposes motion with a force proportional to velocity: \(-c\dot{x}\).

    Newton's second law, \(m\ddot{x} = \sum F\), gives the governing differential equation:

    \[ m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = 0 \]

    Natural frequency and damping ratio¶

    Dividing through by \(m\) puts the equation in a standard form that shows up throughout control theory:

    \[ \ddot{x} + 2\zeta\omega_n\dot{x} + \omega_n^2 x = 0, \qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad \zeta = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{km}} \]

    The natural frequency \(\omega_n\) (rad/s) sets how fast the system wants to oscillate. The damping ratio \(\zeta\) (no units) decides how the motion dies out:

    Damping ratio Behavior
    \(\zeta = 0\) Undamped: oscillates forever.
    \(0 < \zeta < 1\) Underdamped: oscillates with a decaying envelope.
    \(\zeta = 1\) Critically damped: fastest return to rest without overshooting.
    \(\zeta > 1\) Overdamped: creeps back to rest without oscillating.

    Simulating it¶

    Equations like this one rarely get solved by hand in practice. A computer steps them forward in time instead. Rewrite the second-order equation as two first-order ones,

    \[ \dot{x} = v, \qquad \dot{v} = -\frac{c\,v + k\,x}{m}, \]

    and advance \((x, v)\) by a small time step \(\Delta t\) over and over. The widget below does exactly that with the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method (RK4), using \(\Delta t = 1\text{ ms}\).
