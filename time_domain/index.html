    1. Time Domain and State Space¶

    Control theory is about making physical things behave the way you want: a motor that holds its speed, a robot joint that reaches its target without overshooting, an arm that tracks a path. Before we can control anything, we need to describe how it moves on its own. This chapter builds that description in the time domain: how a system's variables evolve from one instant to the next.

    By the end of the chapter you'll be able to write the equations of motion for simple systems, read their behavior from a few parameters, put them in the matrix form that every later chapter (and every robotics library) uses, and simulate them in code without the simulation blowing up.

    Dynamic systems¶

    A dynamic system is one whose output depends on its history, not just on its current input. Push a shopping cart and let go: it keeps rolling. Its speed right now depends on every push it has received, minus whatever friction has taken away since.

    We describe dynamic systems with differential equations, which relate a quantity to its rates of change. Three words come up constantly:

    • The input \(u(t)\) is what we can act on: a force, a voltage, a torque.
    • The output \(y(t)\) is what we measure or care about: a position, a speed.
    • The state is the smallest set of variables that, together with future inputs, fully determines the future.

    For software engineers

    Think of the state as everything you'd need to serialize to pause a simulation and resume it later with identical results. The differential equation is the update rule that advances it.

    First-order systems¶

    The simplest dynamic systems have a single state variable and are described by a first-order differential equation: one that involves only the first derivative.

    Example: a cart with drag¶

    A cart of mass \(m\) rolls along a track. You push it with force \(u\), and viscous drag resists it with a force proportional to its speed, \(-bv\). Newton's second law gives

    \[ m\dot{v} + bv = u \]

    Suppose the cart starts at rest and at \(t = 0\) you start pushing with a constant force \(u_0\), a step input. The solution is

    \[ v(t) = \frac{u_0}{b}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \qquad \tau = \frac{m}{b} \]

    Two numbers capture everything about this response:

    • Steady-state value \(u_0/b\): where the speed ends up. Drag eventually balances the push.
    • Time constant \(\tau\) (seconds): how fast it gets there. A heavier cart (larger \(m\)) responds more slowly; more drag (larger \(b\)) makes it settle faster, at a lower speed.

    The time constant has a precise meaning:

    Time elapsed Fraction of final value reached
    \(\tau\) 63%
    \(2\tau\) 86%
    \(3\tau\) 95%
    \(4\tau\) 98%

    A useful rule of thumb: a first-order system has effectively finished responding after about four time constants. Many things behave this way: a cup of coffee cooling, a capacitor charging, a motor spinning up against friction.

    Second-order systems¶

    Add a spring to the cart and things get more interesting. The system can now store energy in two places, as motion and as spring stretch, and trade it back and forth. That trade is what makes oscillation possible.

    The mass-spring-damper¶

    A block of mass \(m\) slides on a frictionless surface. A spring with stiffness \(k\) connects it to a wall, a damper (think of a shock absorber) with coefficient \(b\) resists its motion, and we can push on it with a force \(u\).

    Let \(x(t)\) be the block's displacement from its resting position. Three forces act on it:

    • The spring pulls it back toward rest with a force proportional to displacement: \(-kx\).
    • The damper opposes motion with a force proportional to velocity: \(-b\dot{x}\).
    • The input pushes it: \(u\).

    Newton's second law, \(m\ddot{x} = \sum F\), gives the governing equation:

    \[ m\ddot{x} + b\dot{x} + kx = u \]

    This is a second-order equation: it involves \(\ddot{x}\). It needs two initial conditions, a starting position and a starting velocity, so the system has two state variables. The mass-spring-damper is worth understanding deeply. A robot joint driven by a motor under feedback control behaves, to a good approximation, exactly like this.

    Natural frequency and damping ratio¶

    Dividing through by \(m\) puts the equation in a standard form that shows up throughout control theory:

    \[ \ddot{x} + 2\zeta\omega_n\dot{x} + \omega_n^2 x = \frac{u}{m}, \qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad \zeta = \frac{b}{2\sqrt{km}} \]

    The natural frequency \(\omega_n\) (rad/s) sets how fast the system wants to oscillate. The damping ratio \(\zeta\) (no units) decides how the motion dies out:

    Damping ratio Behavior
    \(\zeta = 0\) Undamped: oscillates forever.
    \(0 < \zeta < 1\) Underdamped: oscillates with a decaying envelope.
    \(\zeta = 1\) Critically damped: fastest return to rest without overshooting.
    \(\zeta > 1\) Overdamped: creeps back to rest without oscillating.

    When the system is underdamped, it actually oscillates at the damped natural frequency \(\omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\), a little slower than \(\omega_n\).

    Reading a step response¶

    Apply a constant force and an underdamped system rises, overshoots, rings, and settles. Engineers summarize that shape with a few standard numbers, and each one maps back to \(\omega_n\) and \(\zeta\):

    • Rise time \(t_r\), from 10% to 90% of the final value: \(t_r \approx 1.8/\omega_n\). Higher natural frequency means a faster system.
    • Overshoot \(M_p\), how far it goes past the final value as a fraction of that value: \(M_p = e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\). Overshoot depends only on the damping ratio.
    • Settling time \(t_s\), until it stays within 1% of the final value: \(t_s \approx 4.6/(\zeta\omega_n)\). The product \(\zeta\omega_n\) sets how fast the oscillation's envelope decays.

    The overshoot formula is worth getting a feel for:

    \(\zeta\) 0.1 0.3 0.5 0.7 1.0
    Overshoot 73% 37% 16% 4.6% 0%

    These formulas turn vague requirements into numbers. "Rise in under half a second and overshoot by no more than 5%" becomes "\(\omega_n \gtrsim 3.6\) rad/s and \(\zeta \gtrsim 0.7\)." Choosing controller gains to hit targets like these is the subject of Chapter 3.

    Impulse response¶

    A step is a sustained push. An impulse is the opposite: a sharp strike, a large force over a very short time, like a hammer tap. What matters is the impulse \(J\), the force integrated over the strike. It changes the block's velocity instantly by \(\Delta v = J/m\) without moving it.

    Starting from rest, an underdamped mass-spring-damper struck with impulse \(J\) responds with

    \[ x(t) = \frac{J}{m\,\omega_d}\, e^{-\zeta\omega_n t} \sin(\omega_d t) \]

    a sine wave at \(\omega_d\) inside a decaying exponential envelope. The impulse response is the system's fingerprint: Chapter 2 will show that it encodes everything about how a linear system responds to any input.

    Try it¶

    The block has mass \(m = 1\text{ kg}\). Press Impulse to strike it. Here \(J = 1.5\text{ N·s}\), so each strike adds \(1.5\) m/s to its velocity. Change \(k\) and \(b\) at any time, even mid-swing, and watch how \(\zeta\) changes the response.

    • Set \(b = 0\). The block oscillates forever.
    • For \(k = 10\) N/m, what value of \(b\) gives critical damping? Check your answer against \(\zeta = b/(2\sqrt{km})\).
    • With \(b = 0\), press Impulse repeatedly in time with the swing. The amplitude grows: this is resonance.

    State space¶

    Solving differential equations by hand works for textbook examples, but a robot arm has dozens of coupled equations. We need a uniform representation that a computer can handle. That representation is state space, and it turns calculus into linear algebra.

    From one \(N\)th-order equation to \(N\) first-order equations¶

    The trick is to give every derivative but the highest its own name. For the mass-spring-damper, define two state variables:

    \[ x_1 = x \quad \text{(position)}, \qquad x_2 = \dot{x} \quad \text{(velocity)} \]

    Then the single second-order equation becomes two first-order ones:

    \[ \dot{x}_1 = x_2, \qquad \dot{x}_2 = -\frac{k}{m}x_1 - \frac{b}{m}x_2 + \frac{1}{m}u \]

    The first equation is just the definition of velocity. The second is Newton's law, solved for acceleration. The same recipe works for any order: an \(N\)th-order equation becomes \(N\) first-order equations, one per derivative.

    The matrix form¶

    Stack the state variables into a state vector \(\mathbf{x} = [x_1,\ x_2]^T\) (bold, to tell it apart from the displacement \(x\)) and the equations become one matrix equation:

    \[ \dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\mathbf{x} + Bu, \qquad y = C\mathbf{x} + Du \]
    \[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -k/m & -b/m \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/m \end{bmatrix}, \qquad C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad D = 0 \]

    Each matrix has a job:

    • \(A\) (the system matrix) describes how the state evolves on its own. All of the system's natural behavior, its frequencies and damping, lives in \(A\).
    • \(B\) (the input matrix) describes how inputs push the state.
    • \(C\) (the output matrix) picks out what we measure. Here it selects the position.
    • \(D\) (the feedthrough matrix) captures any direct path from input to output. It's zero for most mechanical systems.

    This form scales without changing shape. A 7-joint robot arm linearized around a pose has a 14-element state vector (seven angles, seven velocities) and a \(14 \times 14\) \(A\) matrix, but the equation is still \(\dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\mathbf{x} + Bu\).

    For software engineers

    State space is the representation your code will use. In C++ with Eigen, the mass-spring-damper is a few lines:

    #include <Eigen/Dense>

const double m = 1.0, b = 1.0, k = 10.0;
Eigen::Matrix2d A;
A << 0.0,    1.0,
     -k / m, -b / m;
const Eigen::Vector2d B(0.0, 1.0 / m);

Eigen::Vector2d x(1.0, 0.0);  // state: [position, velocity]
const double dt = 0.001, u = 0.0;
for (int i = 0; i < 10000; ++i) {
  x += dt * (A * x + B * u);  // one forward Euler step (see "Discrete time" below)
}

    Swap in different matrices and the same loop simulates a different system.

    Linearization¶

    Real systems are rarely linear. Robot arms certainly aren't: gravity's effect on a joint depends on the sine of its angle. In general, a system's state evolves according to some nonlinear function,

    \[ \dot{\mathbf{x}} = f(\mathbf{x}, u) \]

    and the matrix form above doesn't directly apply. But zoom in close enough to any smooth curve and it looks like a straight line. Linearization applies that idea to dynamic systems.

    Equilibrium points¶

    An equilibrium is a state \(\bar{\mathbf{x}}\) and input \(\bar{u}\) where nothing changes: \(f(\bar{\mathbf{x}}, \bar{u}) = 0\). Near an equilibrium, write the state as a small deviation \(\delta\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}}\) and the input as \(\delta u = u - \bar{u}\). A first-order Taylor expansion gives a linear system for the deviations:

    \[ \delta\dot{\mathbf{x}} \approx A\,\delta\mathbf{x} + B\,\delta u, \qquad A = \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\right|_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}, \bar{u}}, \qquad B = \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\right|_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}, \bar{u}} \]

    \(A\) and \(B\) are matrices of partial derivatives, called Jacobians, evaluated at the equilibrium. (Jacobians return in Chapter 4, where they relate joint motion to hand motion.)

    Example: a pendulum¶

    A rod of length \(l\) with a mass \(m\) at its tip swings about a motor-driven pivot. With viscous friction \(b\) at the pivot and motor torque \(\tau\), its equation of motion is

    \[ ml^2\ddot{\theta} + b\dot{\theta} + mgl\sin\theta = \tau \]

    The \(\sin\theta\) makes it nonlinear. With state \(\mathbf{x} = [\theta,\ \dot{\theta}]^T\) and input \(u = \tau\),

    \[ f(\mathbf{x}, u) = \begin{bmatrix} \dot{\theta} \\ \dfrac{u - b\dot{\theta} - mgl\sin\theta}{ml^2} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\dfrac{g}{l}\cos\bar{\theta} & -\dfrac{b}{ml^2} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \dfrac{1}{ml^2} \end{bmatrix} \]

    With zero torque, the pendulum has two equilibria:

    • Hanging down (\(\bar{\theta} = 0\)): \(\cos\bar{\theta} = 1\), so the bottom-left entry of \(A\) is \(-g/l\). Compare with the mass-spring-damper's \(-k/m\): gravity acts like a spring pulling the pendulum back, and it swings at \(\omega_n = \sqrt{g/l}\).
    • Balanced upright (\(\bar{\theta} = \pi\)): \(\cos\bar{\theta} = -1\), so the entry flips to \(+g/l\). That's a "negative spring" that pushes the pendulum away from equilibrium. The slightest disturbance and it falls. Chapter 2 gives the precise test for this kind of instability.

    Linearization is only an approximation, accurate for small deviations. Replacing \(\sin\theta\) with \(\theta\) is off by about 1% at 14° and about 5% at 30°. That's why controllers designed with linear tools work best when they keep the system near its operating point, and why a robot controller often re-linearizes as the arm moves.

    Discrete time¶

    So far time has been continuous. Computers aren't: they do things in steps. That matters in two places.

    • Simulation: a simulator advances the state by a time step \(\Delta t\) at a time.
    • Control: a controller reads its sensors, computes a command, and sends it to the motors once per cycle, every \(T\) seconds. Between updates the command is held constant, which is called a zero-order hold.

    Both raise the same question: what happens when you replace smooth time with steps?

    Forward Euler¶

    The simplest way to step a system forward is to assume the derivative stays constant over one step:

    \[ \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k + \Delta t\, f(\mathbf{x}_k, u_k) \]

    This is the forward Euler method, and it's the line inside the loop of the Eigen example above. For a linear system it becomes a matrix update:

    \[ \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = (I + \Delta t\, A)\,\mathbf{x}_k + \Delta t\, B\, u_k \]

    It's simple, and it can fail badly.

    Time-step stability¶

    Apply forward Euler to the cart with drag, with no input: \(\dot{v} = -v/\tau\). Each step multiplies the speed by the same factor:

    \[ v_{k+1} = \left(1 - \frac{\Delta t}{\tau}\right) v_k \]

    The real cart's speed decays smoothly to zero. The simulated one does something that depends on the step size:

    • \(\Delta t < \tau\): the factor is between 0 and 1, so the speed decays. Good.
    • \(\tau < \Delta t < 2\tau\): the factor is negative, so the simulated speed flips sign every step. The simulation oscillates, even though the real system can't.
    • \(\Delta t > 2\tau\): the factor's magnitude exceeds 1, so the simulated speed grows without bound. The simulation is numerically unstable.

    Oscillating systems are even less forgiving. For the mass-spring-damper, forward Euler is stable only when

    \[ \Delta t < \frac{2\zeta}{\omega_n} \]

    With no damping (\(\zeta = 0\)), no time step is small enough: every step adds a little energy, and the oscillation grows forever. For the widget's default settings (\(k = 10\) N/m, \(b = 1\) N·s/m) the limit is \(\Delta t < 0.1\) s, much less than the period of the oscillation.

    The lesson: the time step must be small compared to the system's fastest time scale, its smallest \(\tau\) or \(1/\omega_n\). Stiff springs and light masses make time scales short, which is why physics engines struggle with stiff contacts.

    Better integrators¶

    Forward Euler is rarely the right choice in practice.

    • Runge-Kutta 4 (RK4) evaluates the derivative four times per step and combines the results. It's far more accurate, and it handles undamped oscillators as long as \(\omega_n \Delta t < 2.8\). The widget above uses RK4 with \(\Delta t = 1\) ms, far inside that limit.
    • Exact discretization. For a linear system with a zero-order-hold input, the update can be computed exactly using the matrix exponential, \(\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = e^{A\Delta t}\mathbf{x}_k + \dots\). It's exact at any step size, and it's the standard way to convert a continuous-time model for digital control design.

    A controller's update rate raises the same issues, from the other side: a controller that samples too slowly can destabilize a system that would be perfectly stable under continuous control. Chapter 3 shows how the loop rate limits how aggressive feedback can be.

    Summary¶

    • A dynamic system's state is the minimal information needed to predict its future.
    • First-order systems are characterized by a time constant \(\tau\); they finish responding in about \(4\tau\).
    • Second-order systems are characterized by a natural frequency \(\omega_n\) and a damping ratio \(\zeta\). Overshoot depends only on \(\zeta\); speed depends on \(\omega_n\).
    • Any \(N\)th-order linear system can be written in state-space form, \(\dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\mathbf{x} + Bu\), the representation robotics software uses.
    • Nonlinear systems can be linearized around an equilibrium, using Jacobians.
    • Simulating or controlling in discrete time requires a time step that's small compared to the system's fastest dynamics.

    Further reading¶

    • Franklin, Powell, and Emami-Naeini, Feedback Control of Dynamic Systems, covers dynamic response, step-response specifications, and state space in depth. It writes the state equations as \(\dot{\mathbf{x}} = F\mathbf{x} + Gu\), \(y = H\mathbf{x} + Ju\); this course uses \(A\), \(B\), \(C\), \(D\).
    • Spong, Hutchinson, and Vidyasagar, Robot Modeling and Control, is the main reference for the robotics chapters of this course, and its notation is the one we follow.
