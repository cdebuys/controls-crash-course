    Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors¶

    Multiplying a vector by a matrix usually changes its direction. An eigenvector of \(A\) is a special vector whose direction doesn't change; it only gets scaled:

    \[ A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v} \]

    The scale factor \(\lambda\) is the eigenvalue that goes with \(\mathbf{v}\).

    Finding them¶

    Rearranging gives \((A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = 0\). A nonzero \(\mathbf{v}\) can only solve this if \(A - \lambda I\) is singular, so the eigenvalues are the roots of

    \[ \det(A - \lambda I) = 0 \]

    This is a polynomial in \(\lambda\), the characteristic polynomial. An \(n \times n\) matrix has \(n\) eigenvalues (counting repeats). They can be complex; for a real matrix, complex eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs. Each eigenvector lies in the null space of \(A - \lambda I\).

    Example¶

    \[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det(A - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 + 3\lambda + 2 = (\lambda + 1)(\lambda + 2) \]

    So \(\lambda_1 = -1\) and \(\lambda_2 = -2\). Solving \((A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = 0\) for each gives

    \[ \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} \]

    The figure shows \(A\) acting on both eigenvectors and on one other vector, \(\mathbf{w} = [1,\ 0]^T\). Each eigenvector stays on its own line; the eigenvalues here are negative, so both flip. \(\mathbf{w}\) lands on a different line.

    Why they matter¶

    Along an eigenvector, a matrix acts like plain multiplication by a number. That makes eigenvalues the key to systems of differential equations: in Differential Equations, each eigenvalue becomes an exponential \(e^{\lambda t}\) in the solution.

    In Eigen: Eigen::EigenSolver<Eigen::MatrixXd> solver(A); solver.eigenvalues();
